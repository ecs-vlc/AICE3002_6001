\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Recap: Singular Value Decomposition and its applications}
Let's now change direction --- we're going to look at an early success story resulting from using some differentiation and the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).
\\[1em]
For complex $\bm A:$ \\
\begin{align*}
\bm A &= \bm U \bm \Sigma \bm V^*
\end{align*}
where $\bm V^*$ is the \emph{conjugate transpose} of $\bm V$.\\[1em]
For real $\bm A:$ \\
\begin{align*}
\bm A &= \bm U \bm \Sigma \bm V^\top
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Recap: Singular Value Decomposition and its applications}
\begin{itemize}
	\item SVD has many uses:
	\begin{itemize}
		\uncover<+->{
		\item Computing the Eigendecomposition:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Eigenvectors of $\bm A\bm A^\top$ are columns of $\bm U$,
			\item Eigenvectors of $\bm A^\top\bm A$ are columns of $\bm V$,
			\item and the non-zero values of $\bm\Sigma$ are the square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues of both $\bm A\bm A^\top$ and $\bm A^\top\bm A$.
		\end{itemize}}
		\uncover<+->{
		\item Dimensionality reduction
		\begin{itemize}
			\item ...use to compute PCA
		\end{itemize}}
		\uncover<+->{
		\item Computing the Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse
		\begin{itemize}
			\item for real $\bm A$: $\bm A^+ = \bm V \bm\Sigma^+ \bm U^\top$ where $\bm\Sigma^+$ is formed by taking the reciprocal of every non-zero diagonal element and transposing the result.
		\end{itemize}}
		\uncover<+->{
		\item<+-> Low-rank approximation and matrix completion
		\begin{itemize}
			\item if you take the $\rho$ columns of $\bm U$, and the $\rho$ rows of $\bm V^\top$ corresponding to the $\rho$ largest singular values, you can form the matrix $\bm A_\rho = \bm U_\rho \bm \Sigma_\rho \bm V^\top_\rho$ which will be the $best$ rank-$\rho$ approximation of the original $\bm A$ in terms of the Frobenius norm.
		\end{itemize}}
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{The Netflix Challenge}
\framesubtitle{A million-dollar prediction problem}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> In 2006, Netflix released a large collection of customer ratings and offered \$1 million for improving its recommender system by 10\%.
	\item<+-> The released data contained:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> $100{,}480{,}507$ ratings;
		\item<+-> from $480{,}189$ customers;
		\item<+-> for $17{,}770$ films.
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> Given a customer, a film and everything that customer had rated previously: \textbf{what rating would they give the film?}
\end{itemize}
\blfootnote{\tiny Simon Funk, \href{https://sifter.org/~simon/journal/20061211.html}{Netflix Update: Try This at Home}, 2006.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{The Netflix Challenge}
\framesubtitle{Why might a low-rank model work?}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Films are not completely unrelated objects.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> They have properties such as genre, style, cast, age and overall popularity.
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> Users are not completely unrelated either.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> They have different preferences for those properties, as well as different tendencies in how they use the rating scale.
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> Perhaps both films and users can be represented using a relatively small number of \emph{latent factors}.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> The factors are learned from the ratings; we do not tell the model that one of them should mean ``likes action films''.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Learning a low-rank model from observed ratings}
\framesubtitle{SVD gives us the idea, but not quite the solution}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> If the entire matrix $\bm R$ were known, a truncated SVD would give its best low-rank approximation in terms of the Frobenius norm.
	\item<+-> But most entries of $\bm R$ are unknown, and treating them as zeros would solve the wrong problem.
	\item<+-> Instead, construct two smaller matrices:
	\begin{align*}
		\bm P&\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\rm users}\times k}, &
		\bm Q&\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\rm films}\times k},
	\end{align*}
	where $k$ is the number of latent factors.
	\item<+-> The predicted rating is the dot product of a user's factors and a film's factors:
	\begin{align*}
		\hat r_{ui}=\bm p_u^\top\bm q_i.
	\end{align*}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Learning a low-rank model from observed ratings}
\framesubtitle{Only score the ratings that we actually observed}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Let $\Omega$ be the set of user--film pairs for which Netflix supplied a rating.
	\item<+-> We can learn $\bm P$ and $\bm Q$ by minimising:
	\begin{align*}
		\mathcal{L}(\bm P,\bm Q)
		&=\sum_{(u,i)\in\Omega}\Bigl[
		\left(r_{ui}-\bm p_u^\top\bm q_i\right)^2 \\
		&\qquad+\lambda\left(\lVert\bm p_u\rVert^2+\lVert\bm q_i\rVert^2\right)\Bigr].
	\end{align*}
	\item<+-> This is usually called \emph{matrix factorisation} rather than SVD:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> there are no orthogonality constraints or explicit singular values;
		\item<+-> and the loss is evaluated only over $\Omega$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Learning a low-rank model from observed ratings}
\framesubtitle{Can we derive an update for one rating?}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Take one observed rating $(u,i)\in\Omega$ and define its residual:
	\begin{align*}
		e_{ui}=r_{ui}-\bm p_u^\top\bm q_i.
	\end{align*}
	\item<+-> Its regularised loss is:
	\begin{align*}
		\ell_{ui}=e_{ui}^2+\lambda\left(\lVert\bm p_u\rVert^2+\lVert\bm q_i\rVert^2\right).
	\end{align*}
	\item<+-> What are $\frac{\partial\ell_{ui}}{\partial\bm p_u}$ and $\frac{\partial\ell_{ui}}{\partial\bm q_i}$?
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> Start with one component, $p_{uj}$ or $q_{ij}$, and use the chain rule.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Learning a low-rank model from observed ratings}
\framesubtitle{The resulting gradients are rather simple}
\begin{align*}
	\uncover<+->{\frac{\partial e_{ui}}{\partial\bm p_u}&=-\bm q_i,
	&\frac{\partial e_{ui}}{\partial\bm q_i}&=-\bm p_u \\[0.75em]}
	\uncover<+->{\frac{\partial\ell_{ui}}{\partial\bm p_u}
	&=-2e_{ui}\bm q_i+2\lambda\bm p_u,
	&\frac{\partial\ell_{ui}}{\partial\bm q_i}
	&=-2e_{ui}\bm p_u+2\lambda\bm q_i.}
\end{align*}

\uncover<+->{Absorbing the factor of two into the learning rate, stochastic gradient descent gives:}
\begin{align*}
	\uncover<+->{\bm p_u&\Leftarrow\bm p_u+\eta(e_{ui}\bm q_i-\lambda\bm p_u), \\}
	\uncover<+->{\bm q_i&\Leftarrow\bm q_i+\eta(e_{ui}\bm p_u-\lambda\bm q_i).}
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Learning a low-rank model from observed ratings}
\framesubtitle{One rating at a time}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Initialise the user and film factors to small values.
	\item<+-> Repeatedly visit the ratings in $\Omega$:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item<+-> predict $\hat r_{ui}=\bm p_u^\top\bm q_i$;
		\item<+-> compute the residual $e_{ui}=r_{ui}-\hat r_{ui}$;
		\item<+-> update only $\bm p_u$ and $\bm q_i$.
	\end{enumerate}
	\item<+-> Each update touches only two vectors of length $k$.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> A complete pass costs $\mathcal{O}(k|\Omega|)$ rather than operating on all $8.5$ billion possible entries.
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> In an implementation, compute the two updates simultaneously or retain the old value of $\bm p_u$ for the update to $\bm q_i$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Learning a low-rank model from observed ratings}
\framesubtitle{Don't make the latent factors explain the obvious stuff}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Some films are broadly popular, and some users give consistently higher ratings than others.
	\item<+-> Add a global mean and user and film biases:
	\begin{align*}
		\hat r_{ui}=\mu+b_u+b_i+\bm p_u^\top\bm q_i.
	\end{align*}
	\item<+-> The latent factors can then focus on interactions between particular kinds of users and films.
	\item<+-> Regularisation is especially important for users and films with very few ratings.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{The Netflix Challenge}
\framesubtitle{Where does the Simon Funk SVD fit?}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Simon Funk publicly described a remarkably simple gradient-based factorisation during the competition.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> His implementation trained one latent feature at a time, cached residuals and added shrinkage to reduce overfitting.
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> It made the basic idea accessible and demonstrated that it could be both fast and memory efficient.
	\item<+-> Matrix-factorisation models became important ingredients in leading solutions...
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> ...but the 2009 winning system was a large blend of many predictors, not Funk's algorithm alone.
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\blfootnote{\tiny Sources: \href{https://sifter.org/~simon/journal/20061211.html}{Funk (2006)}; \href{https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/statistics/dmsl/GrandPrize2009_BPC_BellKor.pdf}{Koren, The BellKor Solution to the Netflix Grand Prize (2009)}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{The Netflix Challenge}
\framesubtitle{The data were anonymous --- weren't they?}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Netflix did not publish customer names or contact details.
	\item<+-> Each released record did, however, contain:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> a pseudonymous customer identifier;
		\item<+-> the film that they rated;
		\item<+-> the rating;
		\item<+-> and the date on which they rated it.
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> Is removing a person's name sufficient to make their data anonymous?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{The Netflix Challenge}
\framesubtitle{Sparse behaviour can form a fingerprint}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> An individual is unlikely to have rated exactly the same unusual combination of films, on roughly the same dates, as many other people.
	\item<+-> Narayanan and Shmatikov showed how a little outside knowledge could be linked to the released Netflix records.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> Public ratings from IMDb supplied examples of that outside knowledge.
		\item<+-> The matching procedure tolerated approximate ratings, incorrect entries and errors in rating dates.
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> Once a record was linked to a known person, their other ratings could reveal preferences that they had never made public.
\end{itemize}
\blfootnote{\tiny Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov, \href{https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~shmat/shmat_oak08netflix.pdf}{Robust De-anonymization of Large Sparse Datasets}, 2008.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{The Netflix Challenge}
\framesubtitle{Pseudonymisation is not necessarily anonymisation}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> Whether a dataset is anonymous depends on more than whether obvious identifiers have been removed.
	\item<+-> We must also ask:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> How distinctive is each person's pattern of behaviour?
		\item<+-> What auxiliary information might an attacker possess now --- or in the future?
		\item<+-> Which attributes are actually required for the intended research?
	\end{itemize}
	\item<+-> Could Netflix have reduced the risk by coarsening dates, suppressing rare ratings, aggregating records, or adding noise?
	\begin{itemize}
		\item<+-> What would each of these do to the scientific utility of the data?
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{The Netflix Challenge}
\framesubtitle{The two sides of sparsity}
\begin{itemize}
	\item<+-> The rating matrix was sparse, so we could train efficiently by updating only the two factor vectors involved in each observed rating.
	\item<+-> Individual behaviour was also sparse, so a small set of unusual ratings could make a person's record distinctive.
	\item<+-> The same property that made the learning problem computationally tractable helped create the privacy risk.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
